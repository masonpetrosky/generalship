## drayton-1863-03-detailed

Detailed report of Captain Drayton, U. S. Navy, commanding' D. S. S. Passaic. U. S. Ironclad Passaic, Ogeechee River , March 1863. Sir: I wrote a short report last evening after my return from the attack on Fort McAllister, and will now make one more in detail. A list of the ammunition expended, of the injuries to the vessel, and an extract from the log I enclose with this, from which latter you will perceive that my vessel was under fire just eight hours. 1 was directly in front of the fort, the guns being, as we looked at them, in the center between high traverses of earth, which were on each side. These, how¬ ever, as we were placed had no effect in protecting either guns or men. The latter never exposed themselves to our fire, usually dis¬ charging their pieces either while we were loading or just as our ports came in line and before the guns were quite ready, the turret being painted black not deceiving them any more than a different color had done in the first attack of the Montauk. I was as close to the fort as the pilot, who is the best on the river (as was proved in this case by my being the only one whose vessel was not aground when the tide fell), would take me, and as high as the Montauk had been except on her second attack, but only for an hour, when, being in 12 feet, as I understand, Captain Worden was obliged to drop down to a position below, where I was yesterday — a rather risky operation, too, in so narrow a channel, where, had the vessel grounded, she must have been exposed below the side armor. This being the case, I am satisfied that 12 feet can not be carried when a vessel is to anchor above where I was. The channel is close to the marsh opposite the fort, and as the piles were only about 400 yards beyond where I was lying I don’t see how more than 200 yards can be gained nearer to the fort by being against them, as the river can’t be crossed, and is very wide; certainly, after discussing the matter with the pilot on the spot, I doubt being able to get nearer than a thousand yards without going above the piles, where the channel crosses over. My distance I judged to be 1,200 yards from the following data: The Xl-inch gun was very carefully elevated by a spirit level to a little less than six degrees; this is, by Ordnance Manual, 1,500 yards, axis of bore 10 feet above the water; mine was about 4, which requires a deduction of 130 yards. Then the parapet of the fort was at least 20 feet above the water, which, requiring about half a degree more, will bring the distance about what I have stated it. Their shot were always less than five seconds in reaching me from the flash— generally four— which would, considering their greater initial velocity, about agree with my calculation. My five-second fuzes, however, usually burst just before reaching, the seven after striking. The pilot called the distance a thousand yards. The fort is very solidly built, with high traverses between the guns, and raised at least 20 feet above the river, and contained seven guns and a Xl-inch mortar. One of these guns was, I think, destroyed, the others used until we were out of range. Immense holes were cut into the earth, the traverses and face much cut away, but still no injury done which, 1 think, a good night’s work would not repair, and I do not believe that it can be made untenable by any number of ironclads which the shallow water and narrow channel will permit to be brought into position against it. The guns are one X-inch, a shot from which 718 SOUTH ATLANTIC BLOCKADING SQUADRON. lodged on the top of our turret, one heavy rifle, about a hundred- pounder, and the remainder, 1 should judge, 32s, with a light gun throwing bolts, which would be aimed at anyone showing, himself on the deck. These, with the mortar, were, however, nothing, as a defense to the river, comparable to the shallow water and piles, as was proved by my being exposed to their tire for eight hours without serious injury, but they answer the purpose, which is simply to pre¬ vent the channel being cleared of obstructions. Our three mortar schooners kept up a tire during the day from about 4,000 yards dis¬ tance, but, so far as I could observe, without the least effect, the shells generally falling short. The firing from this vessel was quite good, and 1 think very few shots missed striking about the parapet after the first hour, as I had carefully corrected each one, up to that time, from outside of the tur¬ ret on deck, where the effect could be well followed. Although the attack was an unsuccessful one, it was certainly not owing to any want of zeal and attention to duty on the part of either officers or men, and I am greatly indebted to Lieutenant-Commander J. N. Miller, the executive officer, as well as to Acting Master S. Huse, who attended under him to the management and pointing of the guns, and whose energy and zeal was very marked on this occasion, as it has been on several others. 1 feel very much, of course, the failure, to a certain extent, of the attack, which you had entrusted to my direction, but am satisfied that the natural obstacles are such as to render another just as little likely to succeed. The experience obtained is worth something in future operations. I only withdrew when all my shell with fuzes long enough to reach (or over five seconds) had been expended, and when the crew were almost beyond further work, having been occupied for eight hours at the guns without even an intermission to eat, and then I should have remained had I seen the least sign of faltering or slackness of fire on the part of the enemy; but, on the contrary, I think that it was, if any¬ thing, more rapid toward the last. The gunboats Seneca , Lieutenant-Commander Gibson; WissahicJcon , Lieutenant-Commander Davis, and Dawn, Lieutenant Barnes, were anchored near the mortar schooners in signal distance of us, and pre¬ pared to give assistance had any been required. Everything about the guns and carriages worked to my satisfaction, except that the box around the XV-inch gun, on examination was found to be almost detached from the side, owing to the breaking of the bolts which secure it to the turret. From its appearance this morning, I should think that it could scarcely stand a dozen more shots. My deck having been very badly injured, will require some repairs before I could, 1 think, safely go outside. The mortar shell which fell on deck over the bread room, would undoubtedly have gone through had it not struck on a beam; as it was, it has completely crushed in the planking at the side of the beam, opening quite a hole through, and had it been loaded with powder instead of sand, might have set the vessel on fire. I have measured a piece of it, and it does not seem to have been larger than X-inch; this certainly does not say much for the strength of the deck, the injury to which has been so much more serious than to that of the Montauh , that I must attribute it to a worse class of iron, unless heavier guns have been mounted since the attack made by Commander Worden. ## drayton-1863-03-08-ordnance

Report of Captain Drayton, D. S. Navy, to the Chief of Bureau of Ordnance, giving experi¬ ence gained by the bombardment. F. S. Ironclad Passaic, Port Royal, March 8 , 1863. Sir: I beg leave to offer to the Bureau the experience gained during the eight hours that this vessel was under the fire of Fort McAllister, on the Ogeechee, on the 3d of March, particularly that which especially belongs to ordnance. The distance from the fort I judged to be about 1,200 yards from the following data: The platform on which the gun is placed being level, I elevated the bore to a iittle less than 6 degrees for the Xl-inch. This threw a shell with 15 pounds of powder (ordinary cannon) onto and over a parapet 25 feet above the water line, the axis of trunnion being a little less than 4 feet from the water. This would, I consider, make the distance between 1,200 and 1,300 yards. In addition, the 726 SOUTH ATLANTIC BLOCKADING SQUADRON. * V shot from the fort struck me in four seconds from the flash, and nearly a second after the report. I am particular in mentioning these facts, as the distance is so important an element in the endurance of the vessel. Both guns worked well, and I could see no signs of giving away in the carriages. That part of the fixtures, however, which I always said was too weak — the box round the muzzle of the XV-inch gun — would have been down in six more fires, as every bolt in the angle irons hold¬ ing it to the turret was broken off in that part attached to the turret. The slide also became useless, except for one elevation after the twen¬ tieth fire. The guns were fired immediately after each other, and I do not see that the greater rapidity in loading the smaller one can be taken advantage of, owing to the delay caused from so frequent turn¬ ing of the turret, and thereby exposing the ports to be entered by shot, which is an important consideration, as the enemy, from all experience so far, almost invariably waits for the port to be opened to him before firing, no coloring of the turret deceiving him as to the side on which the port is, the only way to do which, I am satisfied, is to have an outer covering of some light material simply to obstruct the light and shade. The XV-inch gun was fired at an elevation of rather less than 5 degrees and recoiled so as to bring the muzzle just clear of the con¬ cussion box. In making the rammers light enough to handle, they have been made with very short heads and horns to keep them in posi¬ tion. These last invariably break off, and require to be much stronger. The front part of the head is also very liable to splitting. In only two cases did the small projection on the XV-inch cartridge enter the chamber, and the primer, in consequence, will not explode the charge unless priming powder is used, and the only case in which there was a failure to do so occurred from the captain of the gun omitting it. Some kind of a rammer which can be used with a closed port is, I think, very important. While I was firing at the fort several rifle¬ men came into the marsh and opened fire on the side to which we turned our guns to be loaded, and had I not driven them away with some canister it might have been necessary to keep the shutters closed, which, with the present rammers, is inconvenient. A rammer of Lieutenant-Commander Jeffers’ invention is, I think, very good. Our fire was slower than it would otherwise have been, owing to the necessity of changing fuzes. Almost all of our shells had only one fuze hole, and in it was usually a 5-second fuze. This I had not changed, being told that the distance would not be over a thousand yards. I also ran out of 7-second and 10-second fuzes. In view of the very limited shell room and the difficulty of handling these large shells, I would recommend that those with one fuze hole be withdrawn. When we commenced the action, I had up thirteen shells for the XV-inch. These were fired away in ninety minutes, and I consider this to be about as fast as it will be found they can be.delivered to advantage. The aver¬ age of the whole was ten minutes; that of the Montauh in her three actions the same; of the Nahant thirteen, and of the Patajpsco thirty minutes. The pointing was done for both guns with the Xl-inch, the port of the other being entirely closed by the concussion box. This answered, however, very well, and after the range was once obtained the shot fell most uniformly and with scarce any variation at the desired spot. The X-inch mortar shell, weight without sand 87 pounds, would have gone clean through the deck and into the bread rooms had it not struck partly on the beam and directly where it was supported by an iron knee. As it was, there being a little sea, the bread was all spoiled from water rushing over the deck above the hole. Judging from the blows on the side armor, I should say that at 400 yards it would be entirely penetrated, and I am satisfied that it will be shown on the first occasion of a nearer approach to heavy guns par¬ ticularly that the turret, pilot house, and smokestack have alone suf¬ ficient resisting power. One of our pilots, an Englishman, deserted from Fort McAllister two months ago. The battery was then an Vffl-inch and six 32s. A few days before he left, after a visit from Genei*al Beauregard, two rifles and a mortar were sent down, which were about being mounted, and since then a X-inch has been added, as we have the shot which struck the pilot house, making an indentation of 1 inch, and being caught on the rebound by the rim of the turret. A second one, which struck on the opposite side just as we were getting underway, was broken into small pieces without sensibly jarring the pilot house, as 1 could observe, from being at the time on top of the turret, outside and leaning against it, to shelter myself from the enenry’s shot. The two indentations are exacthr alike as to depth and curve. The whole shot is only flattened rather less than a quarter of an inch. As we were swinging to the flood tide, both the guns were fired so as just to clear the smokestack, and the engineers who were watching the effect report that there was no injurious disturbance to the boilers. It was thought on board of the Montauk that tying the cartridge at both necks had the effect of causing the small projection to enter the cham¬ ber. Such was not, however, the case, nor do the blocks serve any better purpose. There was no inconvenience in the turret from either smoke or noise, at least that was not quite bearable, and even less in the pilot house. Below deck the chimneys of two lamps were broken, but no light was extinguished nor any particular jar felt when the deck was struck or our own guns fired over it, nor was the atmosphere below particu¬ larly oppressive, although at times the smoke was quite disagreeable. The conclusion I have come to, both from my attack and that of the Montauk on Fort McAllister is, that if we expect to reduce heavy earthworks, it must be done as in the attack of fortified places on shore, by a continuous fire by day to destroy, and at night to stop repairs, and this seems to be the opinion of the army officers, some of whom witnessed the affair of the 3d. They also state that enfilading works with such heavy traverses is not more favorable than acting on the front. This being the case, the only question that seems to remain is one of distance, and except for the increased chance of hitting the guns, I rather doubt whether the effect of the shells would not be as great at 1,200 as at 600 yards. In the case of Fort McAllister, there is, however, no opportunity of testing the question, as just beyond where I was anchored the river becomes too shoal to permit a vessel drawing near 12 feet to remain after quarter ebb, and to retire with a falling tide in a narrow channel, by dropping down, was more risk than I was inclined to take without a more important object than the one in view at the time, and, indeed, the pilot would not have taken 728 SOUTH ATLANTIC BLOCKADING SQUADRON. me higher without a positive order on my part to absolve him from censure. No one except myself is possessed of an}r information on which to base the distance from the fort, but 1 send you a statement, given me by Commander Worden, which shows the time he was under tire and the distance as estimated by the eye. We had the same pilot, and from the positions he pointed out to me as occupied by the Mon- tauk , with reference to a target which was on the river bank, I should say that I was nearer, decidedly, when his estimated distance was 1,500 yards, slightly so at the place of his 1,400, and 50 yards short of where he supposed ‘it to be 1,200 yards. From the. report of Commander Downes, of the Nahant , you will see that he used 3 pounds more powder than the regular charge of the XV-inch, as his carriage only allows an elevation of 6 degrees, and he tells me that there was quite a list on the side of fire. Five degrees must have been the full prac¬ ticable elevation, and the JVahant being some distance farther off than the Passaic , would about make an agreement with my supposed ranges. The injury done to my deck and side armor is so very much more serious than any which the Montauk has suffered that I am at a loss to account for it. ' The distance so nearly agreeing leaves only two modes of explanation, one that the iron used on the Passaic is of an inferior quality, the other that the size of the shot was greater than any which struck the Montauk. Out of the four guns of the Passaic and JVahant , two were used up, as regarded a continuance of fire, my XY-ineh from the breaking loose of the box, the Xl-inch of the JVahant owing to the yoke giving way. The XV-inch of the Patapsco was only fired fourteen times and was consequently not much tried. The 200-pounder rifle, however, which was discharged forty-six times, stood well. This certainly looks as if our gun fixtures were not to be depended on for long firing, and I consider it very fortunate that in my case the dis¬ covery was made before going into Charleston Harbor. Your obedient servant, P. Drayton, Captain. Rear-Admiral J. A. Dahlgren, Chief of Bureau of Ordnance , Washington.
